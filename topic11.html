Topic 11 – Monocotyledonous and Dicotyledonous Plants

In modern systematics, the historical classification into monocotyledons (monocots) and dicotyledons (dicots) has been fundamentally redefined by molecular phylogenetics. Monocots represent a strictly monophyletic group that evolutionary emerged through a specialized divergence from within the early dicots. Consequently, the traditional dicots are recognized as a paraphyletic grade rather than a natural group. This is evidenced by the existence of early-diverging lineages, known as basal angiosperms (such as Amborellales, Nymphaeales, and Austrobaileyales) and magnoliids, which are evolutionarily older and branched off before the final split between the monocots and the true core dicots (eudicots).

The most important families of dicotyledons

Asteraceae (Daisy family)

  • Habit & Vegetative organs: Mostly herbaceous annuals or perennials, rarely shrubs or trees. Leaves are highly variable, typically alternate, sometimes arranged in a basal rosette, and lack stipules.
  • Biochemistry: Characterized by the storage of the carbohydrate inulin instead of starch. Often contains specialized secretory structures such as laticifers (producing latex), and frequently produces sesquiterpene lactones and polyacetylenes as defense compounds.
  • Flowers: Flowers are small (florets) and crowded into a compact, flower-like head called a capitulum, which is supported by protective bracts below the capitulum. The capitulum can contain tubular florets (bisexual), ray florets (bisexual, female or sterile), or both together. In the bisexual florets, stamens are 5, with filaments free but anthers fused into a tube around the style. The ovary is inferior, containing a single basal ovule.
  • Fruit: The calyx is reduced within the flower, but during maturation in some plants, it is highly modified into a hairy, bristly, or scaly structure called a pappus, which aids in wind dispersal. The fruit is an achene.
  • Important species:
    • Crops: Sunflower (Helianthus annuus), Lettuce (Lactuca sativa), Artichoke (Cynara cardunculus)
    • Ornamental plants: Chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum spp.), Dahlia (Dahlia spp.), Marigold (Tagetes spp.)
    • Medicinal plants: Milk thistle (Silybum marianum), Purple coneflower (Echinacea purpurea), Chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla)
    • Grassland plants: Oxeye daisy (Leucanthemum vulgare), Dandelions (Taraxacum spp.)
    • Problematic weeds: Canada thistle (Cirsium arvense), Canadian fleabane (Conyza canadensis), Scentless mayweed (Tripleurospermum inodorum)

Papaveraceae (Poppy family)

  • Habit & Vegetative organs: Mostly herbaceous annuals, biennials, or perennials. Leaves are typically alternate, deeply lobed or pinnately dissected, and lack stipules. Tissues are soft and easily broken.
  • Biochemistry: Highly characterized by the presence of a well-developed laticifer system that produces a distinct milky, white, yellow, or orange latex. Rich in diverse and pharmacologically active alkaloids (such as morphine, codeine, and papaverine).
  • Flowers: Flowers are usually large, bisexual. The calyx consists of 2 sepals that typically fall off as the flower opens. The corolla has 4 free petals arranged in two whorls. Stamens are numerous. The ovary is superior with high number of eggs.
  • Fruit: The fruit is typically a dry, dehiscent capsule that opens by pores. Seeds are small and numerous, often containing a tiny embryo and abundant oily endosperm.
  • Important species:
    • Crops: Opium poppy (Papaver somniferum – grown both for culinary poppy seeds and pharmaceutical alkaloids)
    • Ornamental plants: Oriental poppy (Papaver orientale), California poppy (Eschscholzia californica)
    • Problematic weeds: Common poppy (Papaver rhoeas), Fumitory (Fumaria officinalis)


Fabaceae (Legume / Pea family)

  • Habit & Vegetative organs: Trees, shrubs, lianas, or herbaceous plants, both annuals and perennials. Leaves are typically alternate, stipulate, and pinnately or palmately compound, often with terminal leaflets modified into climbing tendrils. Stipules are prominent and sometimes modified into sharp spines.
  • Biochemistry: Characterized by a symbiotic relationship with nitrogen-fixing Rhizobium bacteria housed in specialized root nodules. Often rich in proteins, alkaloids, flavonoids, and non-protein amino acids, with seeds accumulating large amounts of nitrogenous compounds contrary to the seeds of other plant families.
  • Flowers: Flowers are usually bisexual and strongly zygomorphic (bilaterally symmetrical) in a distinctive "papilionaceous" (butterfly-like) structure. Perianth is divided into calyx (5 sepals) and corolla (5 petals). Petals are very characteristic for the whole family: one upper large banner (standard), two lateral wings, and two lower fused petals forming the keel, which encloses the reproductive organs. Stamens are typically 10. The ovary is superior with a single carpel and two or more eggs.
  • Fruit: The fruit is a legume (pod), which develops from a single carpel and typically dehisces (splits open) along both longitudinal sutures at maturity to release the seeds. Seeds store nutrients in two large cotyledons.
  • Important species:
    • Crops: Soybean (Glycine max), Pea (Pisum sativum), Peanut (Arachis hypogaea), Chickpea (Cicer arietinum), Alfalfa (Medicago sativa), Liquorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra)
    • Ornamental plants: Wisteria (Wisteria), Lupine (Lupinus), Laburnum (Laburnum anagyroides)
    • Grassland plants: White clover (Trifolium repens), Red clover (Trifolium pratense), Common vetch (Vicia sativa), Bird's-foot trefoil (Lotus corniculatus)

Brassicaceae (Cabbage family)

  • Habit & Vegetative organs: Annual, biennial, or perennial herbs. Leaves are typically alternate, simple or deeply pinnately lobed, and lack stipules. They are often arranged in a basal rosette during the start of growth.
  • Biochemistry: Characterized by the production of glucosinolates (mustard oil glycosides) and the enzyme myrosinase. When tissues are damaged, these compounds react to form pungent, sharp-tasting isothiocyanates, which serve as a defense mechanism against herbivores.
  • Flowers: Flowers are bisexual, actinomorphic, and arranged in racemes. The calyx and corolla consist of 4 free sepals and 4 free petals arranged in a cross shape (cruciform). The androecium generally consists of 6 stamens (4 long inner and 2 short outer). The ovary is superior and bicarpellate with higher number of eggs.
  • Fruit: The fruit develops from a bicarpellate ovary divided by a persistent membrane. It matures into a specialized dehiscent fruit known as a silique (long and narrow) or a silicle (short and broad).
  • Important species:
    • Crops: Rapeseed/Canola (Brassica napus), Cabbage/Broccoli/Cauliflower (Brassica oleracea), Radish (Raphanus sativus), Mustard (Sinapis alba), Horseradish (Armoracia rusticana)
    • Ornamental plants: Dame's Violet (Hesperis matronalis), Honesty (Lunaria annua) and rock plants (e.g., Aubrieta)
    • Problematic weeds: Shepherd's purse (Capsella bursa-pastoris), Field pennycress (Thlaspi arvense), Thale cress (Arabidopsis thaliana – the last one is also a key model organism in plant biotechnology and genetics)
