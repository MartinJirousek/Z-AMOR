Anatomy and Morphology of Plants

Stem

1. Plant stem and its function

The stem is usually an above-ground vegetative organ that forms the main axis of the shoot system. Together with leaves and buds, it constitutes the shoot of vascular plants and serves as a structural and physiological link between the root system below ground and the leaves, flowers, and fruits above ground. Stems are characterized by the presence of nodes and internodes, which distinguish them from roots. Nodes represent points of attachment for leaves, branches, flowers, and buds, while internodes are the segments located between successive nodes.

The stem develops as part of the shoot system (the above-ground part of the plant), which originates during seed germination from the embryonic shoot known as the plumule. This region contains the shoot apical meristem, a population of continuously dividing cells responsible for the formation of all above-ground organs during the plant's life. As growth proceeds, the shoot apical meristem continuously produces new nodes and internodes, giving rise to the mature stem. While the terminal bud maintains elongation of the main axis, axillary buds develop in the axils of leaves and retain the potential to form lateral shoots. The balance between terminal and axillary growth ultimately determines the architecture of the entire plant. Shoot growth is potentially unlimited and can continue for many years or even centuries in long-lived plants.

The stem performs several essential functions. One of its primary roles is mechanical support, elevating leaves into positions favorable for light interception and photosynthesis while also carrying flowers and fruits. Equally important is its transport function. Water and dissolved mineral nutrients absorbed by the root system are transported through the xylem toward photosynthetic tissues, whereas organic substances produced during photosynthesis are distributed through the phloem to growing and storage organs throughout the plant. Many stems additionally serve as reservoirs of water, starch, and other storage compounds. In certain species, stems contribute directly to photosynthesis or vegetative reproduction.

2. Morphology of the stem

Stem morphology is highly variable and reflects adaptation to different environmental conditions and growth forms. Based on their structure and degree of lignification, stems can be divided into herbaceous stems and woody stems. Herbaceous stems are soft, flexible, and only slightly lignified. In contrast, woody stems undergo extensive lignification and secondary growth, resulting in the formation of wood and bark. Several basic types of herbaceous stems can be distinguished. The most common form is a leafy stem bearing both leaves and lateral buds along its length. A specialized herbaceous stem known as a scape develops from a basal leaf rosette and lacks other leaves along its length. Typical examples include dandelion (Taraxacum) and plantain (Plantago). Another characteristic form is the culm, which is typical of grasses (Poaceae) and possesses conspicuous nodes and internodes. In woody plants, the stem forms the permanent structural framework of the plant body. The main axis is referred to as the trunk, while the lateral woody axes are known as branches. Woody stems are characteristic of trees, shrubs, and many perennial climbers.

An important morphological feature of stems is their pattern of branching. In dichotomous branching, the shoot apex divides into two approximately equal branches. This ancient branching pattern is rare and characteristic of clubmosses (lycophytes). In monopodial branching, the terminal bud remains active and continuously extends the main axis, producing shorter lateral branches below it. This growth pattern is typical of many conifers such as spruce and fir. In sympodial branching, the terminal bud ceases growth, and further elongation is taken over by one or more lateral buds. As a result, the apparent main stem is actually composed of a series of successive lateral shoots rather than a single continuously growing axis.

Herbaceous stems also differ in their orientation and growth habit. Erect stems grow vertically upward and are able to support their own weight. Ascending stems initially grow horizontally or obliquely but become upright toward their tips. Decumbent stems lie on the ground while only their distal portions curve upward. Creeping stems grow horizontally along the soil surface and may develop adventitious roots at their nodes, as in strawberry (Fragaria). Some species produce twining stems, which climb by coiling around a support, as observed in hop (Humulus lupulus). Other climbing plants possess tendrils that attach to surrounding structures and provide mechanical support, as in grapevine (Vitis vinifera).

3. Anatomy of the primary stem

The primary stem represents the young, non-woody stage of stem development formed by the activity of the shoot apical meristem. Its internal structure is organized into three main tissue systems: the epidermis, the cortex, and the vascular cylinder. Although the arrangement of these tissues differs among plant groups, the same basic organizational pattern is shared by most vascular plants.

The outer surface of the primary stem is formed by the epidermis, which constitutes the dermal covering tissue of the stem. It usually consists of a single layer of tightly packed living cells covered by a cuticle. The epidermis protects internal tissues against pathogen invasion and excessive water loss. It commonly contains stomata, which allow gas exchange between the plant and the atmosphere, and may bear various types of trichomes.

The region between the epidermis and the vascular tissues is known as the cortex. The outermost layer of the cortex, the hypodermis, lies directly beneath the epidermis. It is a supporting tissue composed of collenchyma (typically in dicotyledons) or sclerenchyma cells that increases the mechanical strength of the stem while maintaining flexibility in growing organs. Mesodermis lies below the hypodermis and is composed predominantly of parenchyma cells. In green stems, outer mesodermal cells may contain chloroplasts and contribute to photosynthesis. The innermost layer of the cortex forms the endodermis, which separates the cortex from the vascular cylinder. In many stems, endodermal cells contain starch-filled amyloplasts and therefore form a so-called starch sheath. These amyloplasts function as statoliths involved in the perception of gravity and contribute to the negative gravitropic growth of the stem.

Immediately beneath the endodermis lies the pericycle, a tissue that may provide additional mechanical support and participate in the formation of secondary tissues during later stages of development. The central part of the stem contains mainly the vascular bundles, consisting of xylem and phloem. In many species, the center of the stem is occupied by the pith, a region composed mainly of parenchyma cells with storage functions. The arrangement of vascular bundles differs markedly between major groups of flowering plants. In most dicotyledons and gymnosperms, the vascular bundles form a ring surrounding the pith, a configuration known as a eustele. This arrangement clearly separates the cortex from the central pith and provides the structural basis for subsequent secondary growth. In contrast, stems of most monocotyledons possess an atactostele, in which numerous vascular bundles are scattered throughout the ground tissue without forming a continuous ring. As a result, distinct cortex and pith are usually absent, and secondary thickening is generally lacking.

4. Secondary growth of the stem

Most gymnosperms and dicotyledonous plants increase the diameter of their stems through secondary growth. This process is driven by the activity of lateral meristems, particularly the vascular cambium and the cork cambium (phellogen). Secondary growth enables the formation of woody stems, increases mechanical stability, and improves the transport capacity of vascular tissues.

The vascular cambium develops between the primary xylem and primary phloem and forms a continuous cylindrical meristematic layer around the stem. Through repeated cell divisions, cambium produces secondary xylem toward the inside and secondary phloem toward the outside. Secondary xylem, commonly known as wood, usually accumulates in much larger quantities than secondary phloem and gradually becomes the dominant component of the stem. Besides water transport, wood provides considerable mechanical support, allowing trees to reach substantial heights. In temperate regions, cambial activity varies throughout the growing season, resulting in the formation of annual growth rings. During periods of rapid growth in spring, cambium produces large xylem cells with relatively thin walls, known as earlywood (spring wood). Later in the season, smaller cells with thicker walls are formed, producing latewood (summer wood). The contrast between earlywood and latewood creates visible annual rings that can be used to estimate the age of woody plants.

As the stem increases in diameter, the epidermis can no longer accommodate the expanding tissues and eventually ruptures. Protection is then assumed by a secondary covering tissue known as the periderm. The periderm is produced by the cork cambium (phellogen), which generates cork (phellem) toward the outside and phelloderm toward the inside. Cork cells are dead at maturity and their walls contain suberin, a hydrophobic substance that reduces water loss and provides protection against pathogens and mechanical injury. Successive layers of periderm, together with tissues external to the vascular cambium, form the bark. In older stems, repeated formation of new cork cambia may cause the outer tissues to crack and peel, producing the characteristic appearance of mature tree trunks. Because cork tissues are largely impermeable to gases, the bark contains specialized openings known as lenticels. These structures allow gas exchange between living internal tissues and the atmosphere, thereby maintaining respiration in woody stems.

5. Stem adaptations

Although the basic functions of stems are similar in most vascular plants, their structure may be considerably modified in response to environmental conditions. These adaptations enable plants to survive drought, flooding, limited light availability, or other ecological constraints while maintaining transport, support, and storage functions.

Plants inhabiting arid and semi-arid environments often possess succulent stems, which are specialized for water storage. In many cacti and other xerophytic species, the stem becomes thick and fleshy due to the development of water-storing parenchyma tissues. The large water reserves stored within succulent stems allow plants to survive prolonged periods of drought. Leaves are frequently reduced or transformed into spines, which minimize water loss and protect plants against herbivores, while the stem assumes the primary role in photosynthesis.

In contrast, stems of aquatic plants often exhibit adaptations that facilitate survival in water. The most pronounced modifications occur in completely submerged plants. Because water provides mechanical support and is continuously available, their stems often possess strongly reduced mechanical tissues and poorly developed vascular tissues, particularly xylem. The epidermis is usually thin and the cuticle is greatly reduced or absent. Stomata are typically lacking, as gas exchange occurs directly through the plant surface. These stems commonly contain extensive aerenchyma, a tissue characterized by large interconnected air spaces. The aerenchyma facilitates internal transport of gases and reduces tissue density, thereby increasing buoyancy. As a result, submerged stems are soft, flexible, and incapable of supporting themselves outside the aquatic environment. Aquatic and wetland plants with floating leaves or emergent shoots, such as water lilies (Nymphaea) and common reed (Phragmites australis), are generally less specialized. Nevertheless, they also typically possess well-developed aerenchyma, which improves oxygen transport between submerged and aerial organs. In species rooted in waterlogged sediments, these air-filled cavities allow oxygen to reach submerged tissues growing in an environment where soil aeration is severely limited.

6. Stem modifications

In addition to typical stems described above, many plants develop specialized stem forms known as stem modifications.

One of the most important stem modifications is the rhizome, a horizontal underground stem characteristic of many perennial herbs. Unlike roots, rhizomes possess distinct nodes and internodes, bear scale leaves, contain axillary buds, and produce adventitious roots. Rhizomes serve as storage organs, accumulate reserve compounds, and enable vegetative propagation. Their underground position protects them from frost, drought, grazing, and other adverse conditions. During favorable seasons, new aerial shoots develop from buds located on the rhizome. Typical examples include lily-of-the-valley (Convallaria majalis), couch grass (Elytrigia repens), ginger (Zingiber officinale), and iris (Iris ).

Another common stem modification is the stem tuber, an enlarged underground stem specialized for storage. Stem tubers differ from root tubers by the presence of nodes and buds. These buds can give rise to new shoots and entire plants. The potato (Solanum tuberosum) is one of the most familiar examples of a stem tuber.

Some climbing plants develop stem tendrils, slender modified stems that coil around surrounding objects and provide mechanical support. Stem tendrils allow plants to reach well-lit positions without investing large amounts of biomass into supporting tissues. They occur, for example, in grapevine (Vitis vinifera).

In certain species, stems are modified into thorns, rigid and pointed structures that function primarily in defense against herbivores. Unlike spines, which originate from leaves or stipules, thorns arise from stems or branches and contain vascular tissues connected to the stem. Examples include hawthorn (Crataegus) and blackthorn (Prunus spinosa).

Additional stem modifications include stolons, also known as runners, which grow horizontally along the soil surface and produce new daughter plants at their nodes. This form of vegetative propagation is characteristic of strawberry (Fragaria). Such modifications demonstrate the remarkable developmental flexibility of stems and their importance in plant survival, dispersal, and reproduction.
