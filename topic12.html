Topic 12 – Cereals

Cereals

Cereals are cultivated species of grasses, the Poaceae, which represent one of the largest and most ecologically and economically important plant families. With approximately 12,000 species worldwide, grasses dominate many terrestrial ecosystems, including grasslands, savannas, steppes, prairies, and wetlands. The family also includes the world's most important food crops (cereals), forage plants, turf grasses, and bamboos.

Morphology and Anatomy

Grasses are predominantly annual or perennial herbaceous plants, although some members, especially bamboos, develop woody stems. The stem, known as a culm, is divided into distinct nodes and internodes. Internodes are frequently hollow, while nodes remain solid and provide mechanical support. The vascular bundles are scattered throughout the stem and are surrounded by sclerenchymatous tissues. Secondary growth is generally absent. Leaves are arranged alternately and consist of a leaf sheath, ligule, and leaf blade. Auricles may occur at the junction between sheath and blade and are important taxonomic characters. Leaf blades are usually narrow and exhibit parallel venation. Bulliform cells facilitate leaf rolling during drought, thereby reducing transpiration. Many economically important grasses, including maize, sorghum, and millet, exhibit Kranz anatomy associated with C4 photosynthesis. In these species, chloroplast-rich bundle sheath cells surround the vascular bundles and contribute to efficient carbon fixation under high light intensity and elevated temperatures. The root system is typically fibrous and formed mainly by adventitious roots.

Flowers, Inflorescences and Fruits

Grass flowers are highly reduced and adapted for wind pollination. They are grouped into spikelets consisting of glumes and one or more florets enclosed by a lemma and palea. The perianth is reduced to lodicules. Stamens are usually three in number, and the pistil has two feathery stigmas. Spikelets form various types of inflorescences, including spikes, panicles, and racemes. The fruit is a caryopsis (grain), in which the seed coat is fused with the fruit wall.

Major Cereals

Barley (Hordeum vulgare)

Barley is a self-pollinating diploid cereal and one of the oldest cultivated crops. Plants possess medium to long ligules and very long hairless auricles. The spike consists of triplets of one-flowered spikelets at each node. Long awns are typically present on the lemmas.

The wild ancestor, Hordeum vulgare subsp. spontaneum, is native to Southwestern Asia. Cultivated barley includes two major cultivar groups. In two-row barley (Distichon), only the central spikelet is fertile and the grain is preferred for malting, brewing, and whisky production. In six-row barley (Vulgare), all three spikelets are fertile, resulting in higher yields and predominant use as fodder grain.

Rye (Secale cereale)

Rye is a cross-pollinating cereal cultivated mainly as a winter crop. Modern cultivars are diploid or tetraploid and often exceed 1.5 m in height. The leaves have short ligules and hairless auricles. Spikelets are arranged individually along the spike and typically contain three florets, although the terminal floret is reduced.

Rye originated in Western Asia and Turkey and is highly tolerant of low temperatures, acidic soils, and poor environmental conditions. It therefore plays an important role in agriculture of colder regions.

Common Wheat (Triticum aestivum)

Common wheat is a hexaploid species cultivated as both winter and spring forms. Plants often display a characteristic grey-green coloration and may reach approximately 1.5 m in height. Leaves possess short ligules and long hairy auricles.

The spike contains multi-flowered spikelets with usually two to three fertile florets. Awns may be present or absent depending on the cultivar. Due to its high yield and excellent baking qualities, common wheat is the most widely cultivated cereal in Europe.

Spelt (Triticum aestivum subsp. spelta)

Spelt is an ancient hexaploid wheat with lower yields than common wheat. Grains remain enclosed within persistent glumes, making it a hulled cereal.

Spelt was cultivated in the ancient Near East, Egypt, and the Roman Empire. Today it has regained popularity, particularly in organic farming, due to its relatively high disease resistance and environmental tolerance.

Emmer Wheat (Triticum turgidum subsp. dicoccum)

Emmer is one of the earliest domesticated cereals. It is a hulled tetraploid wheat characterized by long awns and caryopses enclosed in glumes. In prehistoric agriculture it was among the most important grain crops. Today it is used for specialty foods and serves as a valuable genetic resource in breeding programs.

Durum Wheat (Triticum turgidum subsp. durum)

Durum wheat is a tetraploid cereal with short, broad spikes and long awns. It produces hard grains rich in proteins and is the primary source of semolina used for pasta, couscous, bulgur, and related products. It is particularly important in Mediterranean agriculture.

Triticale (× Triticosecale rimpaui)

Triticale originated as a hybrid between wheat and rye. It combines the high yield potential of wheat with the resilience and environmental tolerance of rye. Morphological characters are intermediate between the parental species. The crop is used primarily as forage and animal feed.

Oat (Avena sativa)

Oat is generally cultivated as a spring crop. Plants are characterized by loose panicles, long ligules, and the complete absence of auricles. Oats are particularly important in cooler climates such as Canada, Finland, Russia, and the United Kingdom.

The grains contain substantial amounts of proteins, lipids, minerals, and β-glucans. Common oat produces hulled grains and is widely used as animal feed and human food. Naked oat (Avena nuda) produces free-threshing grains and is commonly used for flakes, porridge, and bakery products.

Proso Millet (Panicum miliaceum)

Proso millet originated in Central Asia and is among the oldest cultivated cereals. Plants possess hairy leaves, short ligules, and paniculate inflorescences. The rounded grains are used for millet groats, porridge, and bird feed. The species is valued for drought tolerance and rapid maturation.

Rice (Oryza sativa)

Rice is the staple food for more than half of the world's population and is cultivated mainly in flooded fields throughout Asia. Major cultivar groups include sticky short-grained varieties and non-sticky long-grained varieties. Rice provides a major source of starch and dietary energy worldwide.

Maize (Zea mays)

Maize originated in the Americas and is among the most productive crop plants. Male flowers form terminal tassels, while female flowers are grouped into ears. As a C4 species, maize exhibits high photosynthetic efficiency and is used for food, feed, starch production, bioethanol, and numerous industrial products.

Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor)

Sorghum originated in Africa and is highly tolerant of drought and heat. Plants possess solid stems and may reach up to 4 m in height. The bisexual flowers form a terminal panicle. Sorghum is cultivated for grain, forage, silage, biofuel production, and biogas generation. Its importance continues to increase in regions affected by water scarcity and climate change.
