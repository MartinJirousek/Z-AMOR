Topic 2 – Plant Tissues

Classification of plant tissues based on cell shape and cell wall thickness

Based on the cell shape, plant tissues are classified into two main types: parenchyma and prosenchyma. Based on the cell wall thickness, plant tissues are classified into parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma.

Parenchyma

Parenchyma is a tissue composed of living, thin-walled cells. Their shape can be isodiametric—meaning equal in all dimensions (spherical, cuboidal, as well as lobed or stellate)—or elongated to plate-like (such as certain epidermal and palisade cells). Intercellular spaces are frequently present between parenchymal cells. Parenchyma is a major component of ground tissues and most commonly serves as assimilative (photosynthetic), storing, covering functions.

Prosenchyma

Prosenchyma consists of highly elongated cells with pointed ends. Typical examples of prosenchymatous cells are tracheids within the wood (xylem) of conifers. At maturity, the cell walls of tracheids undergo lignification and the protoplast dies. Prosenchyma can also be formed by living cells, which are easily observed in mosses.

Collenchyma

Collenchyma is closely related to parenchyma and consists of living cells with unevenly thickened cell walls. It is primarily categorized into angular collenchyma, where cell walls are thickened at the corners where multiple cells meet, or lamellar (plate) collenchyma, which features thickened tangential walls (walls parallel to the organ's surface). In young cells, the thin sections of the cell wall can still expand, making collenchyma a common mechanical (supporting) tissue in young, actively growing parts of plant organs. It occurs mainly under the epidermis (hypodermis) in the stems of dicotyledonous plants or within leaf petioles. Collenchyma cells can also contain chloroplasts and carry out photosynthesis.

Sclerenchyma

Sclerenchyma is a mechanical support tissue characterized by uniformly thickened cell walls. These cells develop a thick layer of secondary wall that later lignifies, causing the protoplast to die. At maturity, sclerenchyma cells are dead, often leaving a negligible cell lumen inside. Narrow, sometimes branching channels (pits) form in areas where the primary wall previously contained fields of plasmodesmata. The most common types of sclerenchyma cells are sclerenchyma fibers located in the xylem (wood) and phloem (bast) of vascular bundles, or they may form sclerenchymatous sheaths and strands throughout plant organs. Isolated sclerenchyma cells or small clusters of them found in leaves, stems, or fruits are called sclereids (stone cells).

Classification of plant tissues based on their functions

Based on their function, plant tissues are generally classified into nine groups: meristematic, covering, absorbing, assimilative, storage, mechanical, conducting, aerating, and secretory tissues.

Meristematic tissues

Meristematic cells are typically small, isodiametric cells with thin primary cell walls, a large nucleus, and dense cytoplasm. They contain only small, poorly differentiated organelles and usually lack large vacuoles. Individual cells are tightly packed, with little or no intercellular space between them. Their cytological features reflect their high mitotic activity and capacity for continuous cell division. Within the apical meristem, cells are not distributed randomly but show a characteristic zonation, with different regions contributing to the formation of specific tissues and organs. According to their position in the plant body, meristems are classified as apical (located at shoot and root tips), axillary (situated in leaf axils and giving rise to axillary buds), lateral (responsible for radial growth), intercalary (located between mature tissues, particularly in grasses), and adventitious meristems, which arise at unusual positions during regeneration or vegetative propagation. Based on their origin, meristems may be primary, secondary, or mixed. Primary meristems are derived directly from embryonic meristematic cells and are responsible for primary growth. Secondary meristems originate through dedifferentiation of already differentiated tissues; a typical example is the cork cambium (phellogen). Mixed meristems combine both primary and secondary characteristics, as seen in the vascular cambium. The shoot apical meristem exhibits a distinct zonal organization. In the classical histogen theory, three histogens are distinguished: the dermatogen, which gives rise to the epidermis; the periblem, forming the cortex; and the plerome, producing the central vascular tissues. In modern developmental anatomy, these regions correspond approximately to the primary meristems known as the protoderm, procambium, and ground meristem, which give rise to the epidermis, vascular tissues, and ground tissues, respectively.

Covering tissues

Covering tissues form the outer protective layer of the plant body and separate internal tissues from the external environment. They protect plants against mechanical damage, excessive water loss, pathogen invasion, and unfavorable environmental conditions, while also regulating gas and water exchange and interactions with the surroundings.

Primary covering tissue is the epidermis, which originates from the protoderm and usually consists of a single layer of tightly packed living cells. Epidermal cells generally possess thickened outer walls covered by a cuticle composed of cutin and waxes, reducing water loss. Specialized epidermal cells form trichomes, which may serve protective, secretory, insulating, or absorptive functions. In roots, the epidermis lacks a cuticle and develops root hairs that increase the absorptive surface area. Therefore, the root epidermis fulfills not only a covering function but also an absorptive function, playing a key role in the uptake of water and mineral nutrients.

Secondary covering tissues replace the epidermis during secondary growth. The most important is the periderm, composed of three components: the internal phelloderm, middle cork cambium (phellogen) and external cork tissue (phellem). Cork cells are dead at maturity and possess walls impregnated with suberin, making them largely impermeable to water and gases. Gas exchange through the periderm is facilitated by lenticels, which consist of loosely arranged cells allowing diffusion between internal tissues and the atmosphere. In older organs, multiple periderms and additional dead tissues may accumulate to form bark.

Absorbing tissues

Absorbing tissues are specialized for the uptake of water and dissolved mineral nutrients from the environment. They occur primarily in roots, where they are represented by the root epidermis (also known as the rhizodermis). Rhizodermal cells are typically thin-walled, living cells that lack a cuticle, facilitating efficient water absorption. Many of these cells develop tubular extensions known as root hairs, which greatly increase the surface area available for uptake. Root hairs arise from specialized epidermal cells called trichoblasts and are usually short-lived but continuously replaced near the root apex. In aquatic plants, absorption may also occur through epidermal tissues covering submerged stems and leaves.

The efficiency of absorption is strongly enhanced by symbiotic associations with mycorrhizal fungi. In such associations, fungal hyphae effectively extend the absorptive system beyond the root surface, considerably increasing the acquisition of water and mineral nutrients, particularly phosphorus. Some type of mycorrhizal symbiosis occurs in most terrestrial plants. Through their association with mycorrhizal fungi, plants achieve more efficient acquisition of water and mineral nutrients. As a consequence, the development of root hairs is often reduced, with the absorptive function being partially transferred to the extensive network of fungal hyphae.

Assimilative tissues

Assimilative tissues, collectively known as chlorenchyma, are specialized for photosynthesis and represent the primary site of carbohydrate production in plants. Their cells are living, thin-walled parenchyma cells with numerous chloroplasts. Assimilative tissues are most abundant in leaves but may also occur in herb stems and other green plant organs.

In most dorsiventral (bifacial) leaves, the assimilative parenchyma is differentiated into two types of tissue. The palisade parenchyma consists of elongated, densely packed cells located beneath the upper epidermis and is highly efficient in capturing light energy. The spongy parenchyma is situated closer to the lower epidermis and contains irregularly shaped cells separated by extensive intercellular spaces that facilitate gas exchange. In isobilateral leaves, assimilative tissues may be distributed more symmetrically on both sides of the leaf.

Storage tissues

Storage tissues are specialized parenchymatous tissues that accumulate reserve substances, water, or other compounds required for future metabolic demands They consist predominantly of living cells with relatively thin cell walls and are often characterized by large vacuoles and/or numerous storage plastids. Storage tissues often play an important ecological role by enabling plants to survive periods of unfavorable environmental conditions and to support rapid growth when conditions improve. Depending on the nature of the stored materials, several forms of storage tissue can be distinguished.

Starch-storing parenchyma accumulates carbohydrates in the form of starch grains within amyloplasts and is common in roots, tubers, rhizomes, seeds, and fruits.

Protein-storing tissues occur especially in seeds, where proteins serve as nutrient reserves for germination and early seedling development.

Lipid reserves are also frequent in seeds and certain fruits.

Water-storage tissues, characteristic of succulents and xerophytic plants, consist of large cells capable of accumulating substantial quantities of water within vacuoles.

Mechanical tissues

Mechanical tissues provide structural support and contribute to the maintenance of plant form. They strengthen organs against bending, compression, stretching, and other mechanical stresses generated by environmental factors or the plant's own weight. Two principal types of mechanical tissue are recognized: collenchyma and sclerenchyma. The distribution of mechanical tissues is closely related to the functional requirements of individual organs.

Conducting tissues

Conducting tissues are responsible for the long-distance transport of water, mineral nutrients, and organic compounds throughout the plant body. Together they form the vascular system, which consists of xylem and phloem.

Xylem conducts water and dissolved minerals primarily from the roots to the aerial organs. Its conducting elements include tracheids and vessels (tracheal elements), both characterized by lignified secondary walls and programmed cell death at maturity. Xylem also contains fibers and parenchyma cells that contribute to support and storage, respectively.

Phloem transports photosynthetically produced organic substances, especially sucrose, from source tissues to regions of consumption or storage. Its principal conducting elements are sieve cells in gymnosperms and sieve tubes in angiosperms. The latter are closely associated with companion cells, which regulate their metabolism and function. Phloem also contains storage parenchyma and sclerenchymatic fibers. Depending on origin, vascular tissues may be primary, derived from procambium, or secondary, produced by the vascular cambium during secondary growth.

Aerating tissues

Aerating tissues facilitate internal gas exchange and ensure adequate oxygen supply to tissues, particularly those located in waterlogged or submerged environments. The most characteristic aerating tissue is aerenchyma, which contains extensive intercellular air spaces forming a continuous internal ventilation system.

A less specialized aerating function is performed by the spongy parenchyma of dorsiventral leaves. Its loosely arranged cells and extensive intercellular spaces facilitate the diffusion of carbon dioxide, oxygen, and water vapour within the leaf, thereby supporting efficient photosynthesis and transpiration.

Secretory tissues

Secretory tissues are specialized for the synthesis, accumulation, and release of various substances that perform ecological, physiological, or protective functions. These substances include enzymes, nectar, resins, gums, essential oils, mucilage, latex, and various secondary metabolites.

Secretory structures may consist of individual secretory cells or multicellular complexes. External secretory tissues release substances onto the plant surface and include glandular trichomes, nectaries, hydathodes, and digestive glands of carnivorous plants. Internal secretory tissues retain or transport products within plant organs. These include secretory cavities, resin canals, oil ducts, laticifers, and idioblasts containing specialized compounds.

Classification of plant tissues based on their origin

The tissues described above are so-called true tissues, which are characteristic of higher plants and arise from meristematic cells or meristematic tissues through repeated cell division, subsequent growth, and differentiation. Their cells have a common origin, remain structurally connected, and are specialized to perform specific functions. In contrast, false tissues (pseudotissues) develop through the aggregation or interweaving of originally independent cells or filaments and therefore lack the developmental organization typical of true tissues. False tissues are common in algae, fungi, and lichens, where they may morphologically resemble true tissues but differ in their origin. A typical example is fungal plectenchyma, which may occur in the form of pseudoparenchyma or prosenchyma and is formed by densely packed hyphae.
